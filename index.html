About

Four pharma companies spent billions targeting the same pocket on a pain channel. Then someone solved the cryo-EM structure, and it turned out the drug binds 30 ångströms away from where every model predicted. That's the kind of problem I think about.

I'm a DPhil researcher at the Hong Kong Polytechnic University. Computational structural biology — which really means I take the 3D coordinates from cryo-EM, run molecular dynamics, and figure out what actually happens when a drug candidate meets a protein. Not what the model predicts. What the physics says. I published my first paper on SARS-CoV-2 spike protein mechanisms in the Journal of Biological Chemistry as an undergraduate at HKUST.

Outside the lab, I built AtomBios — because the teams actually making drugs shouldn't need six months of specialist setup to run a binding simulation. I've been selected by the Nobel Foundation, the Shaw Prize, and the Chinese Academy of Sciences. Won gold at Hong Kong's largest deep-tech competition. But the part that matters most is the work: reading structures that explain why things fail, and building tools so others can read them too.

Right now: finishing my DPhil, getting AtomBios to its first pharma pilot, and talking about structures at conferences across Asia.

Viral capsid. PDB render.
"Every drug team I talked to had the same setup. They could see a protein structure. They knew where the pocket was. But they couldn't test what happens when a molecule actually docks — not the static snapshot, the dynamics."
— on why I built AtomBios

So I built AtomBios. One click. Upload a structure, define a ligand, run molecular dynamics. A thousand binding modes tested before a single compound gets synthesised. The kind of simulation that used to take a specialist six months of setup now takes an afternoon.

AtomBios is incubated at Hong Kong Science and Technology Park, with conditional funding approved through the HKSTP Ideation Programme.
